# Calibrated agency: a tool-first RNA design agent that profiles its oracles and gates its own claims

**Manuscript draft (v1, 2026-08-10).** Target journal: *Nature Methods*. All numbers verified against `experiments/results/` verdict files; see `docs/PAPER_FACT_SHEET.md` for the per-number file citations. System name: **RNADesignAgent**.

---

## Abstract

RNA design has been transformed by specialized computational tools, yet no single tool spans the design pipeline, and orchestrating them remains expert manual work. Large-language-model (LLM) agents can in principle coordinate such toolchains, but current agentic systems exhibit three recurrent failure modes: they invoke predictive tools outside their validated regimes, they assert claims beyond their evidence, and reported "improvements" are frequently confounded by unequal sampling budgets. Here we present RNADesignAgent, a tool-first agent platform covering eleven RNA design workflows built on 54 tools and 22 local microservices, and use it to develop **calibrated agency**: before an agent relies on a predictive oracle, it profiles that oracle's capabilities on curated anchor sets; before it acts or claims, deterministic decision and claim gates check the action or statement against that empirical self-model. Capability profiling of three deployed structure oracles across six RNA–protein targets revoked all presumed screening, ranking and docking power (0/18 oracle–target cells passed screening; 0/5 docking cases). A budget-matched, pre-registered self-audit of our own earlier campaigns dissolved two headline claims, including an apparent coordination benefit that proved indistinguishable from doubled sampling (Cliff's δ = 0.278, P = 0.13). The gated agent eliminated false-positive claims entirely (57.9% → 0%) while retaining all true-positive claims on a blinded audit task set, and a generation-layer constraint navigator delivered pre-registered multi-constraint mRNA design profiles where a rule-based executor failed (McNemar P = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹, 30:0 discordant pairs, n = 54). Calibrated agency converts an agent from an uncritical tool user into one that knows what its tools cannot do — a prerequisite, we argue, for trustworthy agentic science.

## Introduction

RNA has become a therapeutic modality of central importance, from mRNA vaccines to small interfering RNAs, guide RNAs, antisense oligonucleotides and aptamers. Designing these molecules is a multi-objective problem: an mRNA coding sequence must balance codon optimality, secondary-structure stability, translation initiation context and UTR-mediated regulation; an aptamer must fold, bind and remain specific; every candidate must additionally satisfy manufacturability and safety constraints. The field has responded with a constellation of specialized computational tools — sequence optimizers, structure predictors, expression and stability models, off-target scanners — each excellent within its remit and silent outside it. No single tool spans the pipeline, and chaining them into a coherent design campaign remains expert manual work that can take days per target.

LLM-driven agents are a natural candidate to automate this orchestration, and recent systems have demonstrated agentic hypothesis generation, data analysis and tool use across the sciences. Yet the current practice of agentic science inherits three failure modes that are rarely measured directly. First, **uncalibrated tool trust**: agents consume the outputs of predictive models — binding scores, folding energies, expression predictors — at face value, without validating whether a given model has any demonstrated power for the task, target family or molecular regime at hand. Second, **claim overreach**: because agents narrate their own results in natural language, statements can drift beyond what the underlying measurements support, and such statements are difficult to audit after the fact. Third, **confounded improvement**: agent systems are frequently evaluated by comparing best-of-pool outcomes across conditions, but agentic strategies typically also *generate more candidates*, so that an apparent optimization or coordination gain may be nothing more than a larger sampling budget. These three failure modes are not hypothetical; as we show below, all three were present in an earlier version of our own system.

Here we present RNADesignAgent, a tool-first agent platform for RNA design, and **calibrated agency**, the design principle that emerged from measuring and correcting these failure modes. In a calibrated agent, every predictive oracle is profiled on curated anchor sets before use, yielding an empirical self-model of what each tool can and cannot establish; every action and every natural-language claim passes through deterministic gates that check it against this self-model; and every comparative evaluation follows a pre-registered, budget-matched protocol so that gains are attributable to mechanisms rather than to sampling. Our key contributions are:

1. **A tool-first RNA design platform.** Eleven domain workflows composed from 54 tools exposed as 22 local microservices, with deterministic pipelines, interface-locked contracts, service pre-flight, and full per-tool-call audit trails. The LLM orchestrates and reviews but never generates sequences directly.
2. **Empirical capability profiling.** A protocol that maps each (oracle, task type, target family) combination to an evidence-backed capability grade — screening, ranking, docking — using assertion-curated anchor sets, producing a reusable capability map for widely used RNA–protein scoring tools.
3. **Decision and claim gating.** A two-layer gate: a DecisionGate forbids actions whose evidentiary basis is absent from the self-model (for example, ranking candidates by an oracle with no measured ranking power), and a ClaimGate degrades or suppresses natural-language claims that exceed the evidence ("no claim without verification"). Gating is deterministic and therefore auditable.
4. **Generation-layer constraint navigation.** A feasibility-first generator (GenNav) that delivers batches of candidates satisfying pre-registered multi-constraint profiles — the regime, we argue, where an agent can demonstrate value beyond a single tool's native reach.
5. **A pre-registered self-audit methodology and its negative results.** Budget-matched controls, blinded adjudication, and a public register of concluded-and-abandoned directions, so that the system's failures are as inspectable as its successes.

## Results

### System overview: a seven-layer tool-first agent

RNADesignAgent is organized as seven layers (Fig. 1). At the base (L0), each of 54 tools — wrapping 20 vendored external research codes (17 as git submodules) — runs in its own environment behind one of 22 local HTTP microservices (ports 8003–8024), so that heavyweight GPU models (RhoFold, Protenix, EGARPS, ODesign), CPU optimizers (LinearDesign, LinearFold, ViennaRNA) and alignment suites (BLAST+, Bowtie2) are invoked through uniform, versioned contracts. Tool specifications (L0), capability wrappers that validate outputs and write per-call audit records (L1), and eleven domain workflows with a fixed generate → evaluate → score → rank skeleton (L2) are all deterministic; a service manager (L3) maps each workflow's declared requirements to microservices, health-checks them, and auto-starts what is missing. The multi-agent execution layer (L4) comprises a PipelineAgent that schedules workflow nodes deterministically, per-workflow SpecialistAgents, a CoordinatorAgent that runs the bounded multi-module coordination (BMC) loop for the two coupled workflows, an advisory QualityAgent that reviews outputs against per-workflow quality profiles, and a SynthesisAgent for multi-node reports. Session memory and workspace management (L5) and the CLI/FastAPI entry façade (L6) complete the stack. Two governance mechanisms cut across all layers: interface locks, which pin every tool and workflow contract to a synchronized documentation and test surface, and the calibrated-agency stack (SelfModel, CapabilityProfiler, DecisionGate, ClaimGate, AuditTrail), which carries the agent's empirical knowledge of its own tools.

Two design commitments distinguish the platform. First, the LLM never writes sequences: every molecule is produced by a validated generator, and the LLM's roles are restricted to orchestration, review and reporting, which makes every design decision traceable to a deterministic tool call. Second, coordination between molecular modules is *bounded by construction*: when modules are designed separately and assembled — a 5′UTR⊕CDS⊕3′UTR mRNA (Type-I, sequence-level coupling at the junctions) or an aptamer docked to a protein hotspot (Type-II, 3D-interface coupling) — the coordination loop has hard round caps, convergence thresholds, and clamped parameter-adjustment ranges (for example, the LinearDesign stability–diversity trade-off λ is clamped to [0, 20]), so that compute cost and behavior remain predictable and auditable.

### The platform produces biologically valid outputs at scale

We first established that the platform's outputs are biologically meaningful across all eleven workflows, using a 210-task benchmark together with designed null models and literature anchors (Fig. 2). 209 of 210 tasks completed successfully; the single failure was a correct advisory abort on a GC-rich siRNA target. Three observations anchor biological validity. (1) On 20 wild-type protein targets, designed coding sequences reached a median codon adaptation index (CAI) of 0.744 (n = 18; two targets exceeded the LinearDesign memory boundary), exceeding wild-type sequences (median 0.730, n = 20), which themselves exceed random synonymous sequences (median 0.674, n = 200; Mann–Whitney P = 5.6 × 10⁻⁷, Cliff's δ = 0.68) — the platform reproduces the direction of natural selection and improves on it, while wild-type and random sequences are indistinguishable on folding energy per nucleotide (P = 0.28), confirming that the CAI signal is not a folding artifact. (2) Inverse folding against target secondary structures recovered the exact target structure for 22 of 25 benchmarks. (3) On a literature panel of IRES elements, the platform's predictor achieved an AUROC of 0.762 (95% CI 0.429–0.98). In the same spirit of boundary-honesty that motivates the rest of this paper, we report the platform's one negative result at face value: small-molecule binding scores on short aptamers performed at chance (AUROC = 0.5), an out-of-training-distribution tool boundary that we registered rather than optimized away. End-to-end, the platform completes full design campaigns in minutes to about an hour per target (Supplementary Table 1).

### Capability profiling revokes presumed oracle powers

The platform's throughput makes it tempting to treat its most sophisticated scorers as oracles for optimization. Before allowing any such use, we profiled the three structure-based oracles deployed in the aptamer workflow — EGARPS native probability, Protenix ipTM, and RhoFold pLDDT — on assertion-curated anchor sets spanning six protein targets (66 sequence entries with binders, negatives and affinity series; 102 docking poses across five RNA–protein complexes with known native structures). Each (oracle, target) cell was graded against pre-registered thresholds for three capabilities: screening (binder-versus-negative AUROC > 0.8), ranking (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.6 against measured Kd), and docking (native pose ranked top-1 in ≥ 2/3 of cases) (Fig. 3).

No cell passed. Screening power was absent in 0/18 oracle–target cells; the best cell (RhoFold pLDDT on PDGF-BB) reached AUROC = 0.8 exactly with a 95% confidence interval of 0.33–1.0, still below the strict threshold. No oracle approached ranking power (best ρ = 0.56). Docking failed in 0/5 cases: the native thrombin aptamer (TBA) pose ranked last of 21 candidates, and the best native rank across all cases was 4 of 18. An independent calibration panel gave the same verdict from the affinity side: across a four-point thrombin Kd series, EGARPS native probability correlated at ρ = 0.40, and binders scored *below* negatives on average (AUROC = 0.356) — the marketed drug pegaptanib scored ~2 × 10⁻⁶ while a 30-nt random RNA scored 0.95 and a scrambled TBA 0.99. In other words, the scoring chain as deployed not only lacks screening power but is directionally inverted on this molecular family. These results do not imply the oracles are broken; they imply that their published validation regimes do not transfer to RNA–protein aptamer complexes, and that any agent using them for ranking or screening without profiling would be optimizing against noise — or worse, against anti-correlated signal. The capability map produced here (Fig. 3a–c) is itself a community-facing output: it defines where optimization claims are currently void and where they may be entertained.

### Budget-matched self-audit dissolves apparent coordination gains

We next turned the profiling discipline inward, onto our own earlier results. RNADesignAgent's bounded coordination loop had previously shown an apparent main effect on the aptamer workflow: BMC×1 raised the EGARPS-component score over serial execution (paired Wilcoxon P = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴, Cliff's δ = +0.46, n = 19 targets) and raised the three-objective hypervolume (P = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴). Two confounds, however, were visible in the design: each BMC round doubles the evaluation pool (8 → 16 candidates), and the headline metric is exactly the oracle that profiling had just decertified. We therefore pre-registered two decisive controls (Fig. 4a). In a budget-matched control (P0), BMC×1 was compared against serial execution extended to the same 16-candidate budget; the coordination advantage collapsed below the pre-registered effect-size gate (δ = 0.278 < 0.3, P = 0.13, 12 cells). In a guided-versus-unguided control (P3), the diagnostic content of coordination was replaced by a fixed resampling instruction with identical mechanics and budget; guidance added nothing (δ = 0.222, P = 0.11). Consistent with these pilots, the full-length mRNA coordination campaign showed no significant effect at any coordination depth on the fixed-reference scalarization (BMC×1 versus serial P = 0.81, n = 20 targets), and no mechanism-ablation test (feedback mode, reference-point form) survived false-discovery-rate correction. A formal self-audit of our two strongest legacy claims — the aptamer coordination effect and a case-study comparison against pegaptanib — using the four-step protocol (declare capability dependencies, re-check against profiles, control confounds, gated re-adjudication) retired both: claim survival 0/2, below the pre-registered 70% threshold (Fig. 4b).

We draw three conclusions. First, the defensible value of bounded coordination is not "coordination gains" but *adaptive budget allocation with auditable, bounded mechanics*; the loop, its clamps (λ ∈ [0, 20], ≤ 24 new candidates per round, Pareto frontier capped at 10) and its traces are retained in the platform on those terms, and we verified the boundedness directly (an extreme λ = 18 initialization is clamped back into range in all trajectories). Second, best-of-pool comparisons without budget matching are uninterpretable in agentic systems, because agentic strategies almost always also sample more; we now treat budget matching as an iron rule for every comparative claim. Third, an ungated pipeline — including our own earlier version — would have issued both retired claims as results. This is the empirical motivation for gating.

### Double gating eliminates false claims while preserving true ones

We evaluated the calibrated-agency stack on a synthetic audit set of 20 design-reporting tasks, run under two arms with identical prompts and the same underlying LLM: an ungated arm and a gated arm in which the DecisionGate forbids actions lacking capability evidence and the ClaimGate enforces "no claim without verification", downgrading statements along a scope ladder (absolute → same-target relative → trend-only). Claims were adjudicated blind to arm by a deterministic adjudicator (Fig. 4c). On the pre-registered endpoints, gating reduced the false-positive claim rate from 57.9% (11/19 claims) to 0% (0/11) — a 100% relative reduction against a ≥ 50% gate — while true-positive retention was 112.5% (9/8; gating also *rescued* one claim the ungated arm had buried). Overreach events (statements exceeding the sanctioned scope) numbered 23 in the ungated arm and zero in the gated arm, a hard guarantee of the DecisionGate rather than a statistical outcome. Notably, the mechanism of gating is abstention and downgrading, not silence: the gated arm still issued claims, but only within validated scope. A pilot of this experiment had initially failed (gating did not reduce false positives at small scale because the LLM, when blocked, substituted an undeclared free proxy), illustrating that gating must close *all* unverified claim paths — a protocol lesson now encoded in the pre-registration template.

Two platform-level governance results complement this. On the 19-task process-aware regression suite, runs with and without the advisory quality-review agent both succeed 19/19, confirming that the LLM advisor is not a hidden dependency for success; and a governance fix informed by two production incidents now guarantees that LLM advisory failures (for example, API outages) can never convert a successful run into a failure — review unavailability is recorded as audit metadata, not as a verdict.

### Generation-layer constraint navigation delivers pre-registered design profiles

Where, then, can an agent demonstrate value beyond a single tool's native reach? Our answer, arrived at through a failed experiment, is *multi-constraint batch delivery*. Single-metric quality (CAI, folding energy) is essentially determined by the underlying generator's settings — within the tool's own range, an agent adds nothing. The defensible agent task is to deliver, per target, a ranked batch of candidates that jointly satisfy a pre-registered constraint matrix (GC windows, homopolymer runs, local structure stems, boundary GC, CAI floors) across multiple design profiles.

A first attempt placed constraint navigation in the selection layer: a fixed candidate pool (315 sequences per target: four LinearDesign λ settings, one RNARL candidate, thirty GEMORNA candidates) was filtered and ranked. This campaign met its end-to-end delivery and constraint-satisfaction gates (constraint rate 1.0; delivery at ceiling), and mechanizing the ranking (system-enforced utility order with the LLM restricted to tie arbitration) cut LLM involvement to three calls with perfect rank correlation (Kendall τ = 1.0). But the campaign failed its diversity gates overall: on insulin the batch pairwise edit distance fell to 0.82% (< 2% required) because admissible candidates saturate a single λ setting; on GFP only two λ gears were usable until a species-matched *E. coli* codon table was passed through (pool pass 0/315 → 12/315). Every residual failure was structural to the generation layer — λ-gear separation, λ saturation, feasible-gear coverage — not to the coordination or gating machinery (Fig. 5a).

We therefore sank constraint navigation into the generation layer (GenNav): a feasibility-first adaptive λ probe (λ ∈ {0, 2, 8, 20, 50}) that establishes which gears are feasible per target before allocating budget; profile-orthogonal generation axes (CAI-max, fractional, floor) that make design profiles separable by construction; a diversity expander enforcing ≥ 3% pairwise distance within delivered batches; and explicit codon-context resolution for non-human expression hosts. Evaluated on 18 targets under pre-registered dual endpoints, GenNav met the primary endpoint decisively: per-profile joint-criteria delivery passed for the gated navigation arm and failed for the rule-based arm in all 30 discordant pairs (McNemar P = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹, 30:0, n = 54 = 18 targets × 3 profiles), with gene-level end-to-end delivery at 1.0 (Fig. 5b). The stricter secondary endpoint — a quality bundle over all arms — did not pass and is disclosed rather than hidden: essentially all quality failures trace to a single target, HER2, for which the feasibility probe found *no* feasible λ gear and the system degraded gracefully by design. GenNav thus both demonstrates the agent's value regime and delineates its current boundary: when the underlying generator family has no feasible operating point for a target, navigation cannot conjure one, and the honest outcome is a declared degradation rather than a silently weaker delivery. Total LLM usage for the 18-target campaign was 43 calls (mean 2.4 per gene), below the pre-registered budget (Fig. 5c).

### End-to-end case studies

Three end-to-end cases illustrate calibrated operation (Table 1). (A) A full-length SARS-CoV-2 Spike mRNA campaign (CDS design × UTR presets, full-length evaluation, bounded coordination) completed in 85 minutes; the best candidate's CDS CAI (0.946) approaches the BNT162b2 anchor (0.952), and under the platform's junction metric the designed candidate and the marketed-vaccine anchor are *both* flagged — a concordance that first taught us to treat that metric as a soft reference rather than a pass/fail gate, and ultimately motivated the validated full-length ddG reporter described below. (B) An anti-VEGF aptamer campaign completed in 47 minutes; its top candidate scores 0.70 on the EGARPS component where the pegaptanib anchor scores ~5 × 10⁻⁷ — a six-orders-of-magnitude gap that, post-profiling, we report *not* as a superiority claim but as the worked example of the self-audit: the oracle lacks screening power on this family, the complexes come from incomparable sources, and the anchor's 2′-fluoro modifications are unrepresentable to the scorer. (C) An NSP12 siRNA campaign with off-target scanning completed in 73 seconds, with all top-five candidates showing zero predicted off-targets against the reference transcriptome. Each case ships with a complete audit trail: every tool call, parameter, and intermediate metric is recorded in the session workspace.

### A quantified failure analysis delineates the system boundaries

Across the legacy platform campaigns (~1,400 runs), we classified every failure (Fig. 6a). The overwhelming majority of failure events were transient and fully recovered by resumable sweeps (188 infrastructure failures and 20 timeouts recovered; six driver bugs fixed in place); 35 final failures remained, and all of them are deterministic boundaries or explicit governance outcomes: ODesign produced zero candidates on ferritin-family targets (24 cells), eight runs of a nominal second-LLM arm failed on an endpoint misconfiguration, two targets exceeded the LinearDesign memory boundary (now fail-fast under an address-space guard), and one quality abort proved correct. Three further boundaries deserve emphasis as scientific findings. First, junction repair has no free lunch (Fig. 6b): three independent pre-registered attempts to repair 5′UTR–CDS junction burial — post-hoc codon shuffling with a stability-weight sweep, a junction-penalized global-fold optimizer (a purpose-built LinearDesign patch that drives junction-window pairs from 43 to 0 without repairing the fragment-fold burial metric, a coordinate-system mismatch), and a generate-and-select route against a validated full-length constrained-fold ddG metric (constraint obedience 216/216; LinearFold-versus-Vienna r = 0.945) — all failed their acceptance gates because opening junctions costs global stability. The ddG metric itself passed validation and is now the platform's reporter; the repair direction is abandoned, and we regard this triple of negative results as a durable contribution to the mRNA design community. Second, cross-campaign memory does not pay (Fig. 6c): a 120-campaign dual-endpoint trial of experience injection cut evaluation budgets by 74.2% (814 versus 3,153 evaluations) but produced no learning-curve improvement (all three seed-group slopes positive; P = 0.91) and no design-quality gain (four metrics, all non-significant) — the strategy was abandoned, and the negative result is registered to discourage repetition. Third, as noted above, HER2-class targets with no feasible generator operating point mark the current reach of generation-layer navigation. These boundaries are not disclaimers; they are the calibrated agent's working knowledge, encoded in its self-model.

## Discussion

In this work we presented RNADesignAgent, a tool-first agent platform for RNA design, and calibrated agency, the principle that an agent must empirically know its tools before it may lean on them. Three findings carry the argument. First, capability profiling revoked every presumed oracle power we tested (0/18 screening cells, 0/5 docking cases), showing that uncalibrated tool trust is not a theoretical risk but the default state of a deployed system — ours. Second, a budget-matched self-audit retired our own headline claims, showing that confounded improvement is likewise a default state of agentic evaluation. Third, the remedies work: deterministic double gating eliminated false claims entirely while preserving true ones, and generation-layer constraint navigation delivered pre-registered multi-constraint profiles with decisive significance (McNemar P = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹) at a cost of 2.4 LLM calls per gene.

A central insight is that the locus of intelligence in a trustworthy agent is not the language model but the *evidence infrastructure* around it: the anchor sets, the capability grades, the gates, the audit trail. This design choice is not merely a practical compromise but a principled response to how agentic systems actually fail: language is where overreach enters, so claims — not just actions — must be gated; sampling is where confounds enter, so budgets — not just outcomes — must be controlled. We also note what calibrated agency is not. It is not autonomous discovery: the system is a scientist-in-the-loop design instrument whose deliverables are candidates, profiles and audit trails, and we would like to emphasize the distinction between calibrated tool orchestration and genuine scientific reasoning. Nor is it a claim that the underlying oracles are weak — only that their power is *regime-specific*, and that the regime must be established before, not after, optimization.

Several limitations mark opportunities for continued development. (1) All validation is computational; the anchors and nulls are literature-derived, and no wet-lab confirmation was performed. The profiling protocol, however, is wet-lab-ready: anchor sets accept new measurements as first-class rows, and capability grades update without code changes. (2) A single LLM backend (DeepSeek) was used for the in-system language roles. Multi-backend replication was descoped by decision: the gated roles are narrow (tie arbitration, claim drafting), the gates themselves are deterministic and model-agnostic by construction, and the platform-level result that quality review is advisory-only (19/19 with and without) bounds the backend's influence on outcomes. (3) The rule-based comparator in the navigation experiment is deliberately simple; the 30:0 discordance should be read as evidence that constraint navigation is necessary, not that every alternative heuristic would fail. (4) The navigation quality bundle fails on targets with no feasible generator operating point (HER2 class); expanding the generator family or the λ lattice for such targets is the next methodological step and is being handled under the same pilot-first discipline. (5) Capability profiles currently cover the three aptamer-chain structure oracles; the platform's remaining predictors (for example, expression and stability models) carry only their literature validation until profiled, and the anchor sets, though assertion-curated and coverage-checked (≥ 4 binders and ≥ 3 negatives per target), remain modest in size; community contribution of anchors would directly strengthen the self-model. Overall, these represent areas for ongoing development rather than fundamental limitations.

More broadly, we believe the artifacts released here — the capability map of widely used RNA–protein oracles, the assertion-curated anchor sets, the budget-matched pre-registration templates, and the register of concluded directions (including the junction-repair and memory-strategy negative results) — are reusable independently of our system. Any agentic pipeline, in any domain, can adopt the same discipline: profile before you optimize, gate before you claim, match budgets before you compare. We anticipate that calibrated agency will become more, not less, important as agents grow more capable, because each added capability enlarges the space of plausible-sounding but unverified actions.

## Methods

### System architecture

RNADesignAgent is implemented as seven layers. L0 (tools): 54 tools, each defined by a Pydantic-typed specification (identity, version, endpoint, capabilities, limitations) and exposed through one of 22 local FastAPI microservices (ports 8003–8024), one per external code family, each with its own environment and local model weights; 20 external research codes are vendored under `external_repos/` (17 as registered git submodules). L1 (capabilities): thin wrappers that validate tool outputs against their specifications and write audit records (unique per-parameter result keys, per-call timings) into the shared state. L2 (workflows): eleven domain workflows (mRNA CDS design, full-length mRNA design, mRNA sequence scoring, siRNA design, sgRNA design, ASO target screening, IRES prediction, RNA aptamer design, RNA secondary-structure design, RNA tertiary-structure design, RNA–small-molecule interaction), all subclasses of a base workflow enforcing a generate → evaluate → score → rank skeleton with progress events and partial-rerun support. L3 (service orchestration): a service manager mapping each workflow's declared `required_capabilities` to microservices, with file-locked, health-check-gated auto-start that distinguishes a busy service (port listening, health probe starved) from a dead one. L4 (multi-agent execution): a deterministic PipelineAgent scheduling pipeline nodes; per-workflow SpecialistAgents owning retry decisions; a CoordinatorAgent running the bounded coordination loop; an advisory QualityAgent issuing per-workflow quality reports (three deterministic workflows are explicitly exempt, since retrying fixed inputs cannot change outputs); and a SynthesisAgent for multi-node reports. L5 (session/workspace): per-session workspaces with context-propagated session IDs and cross-turn result history. L6 (entry): a form-driven CLI and FastAPI façade through a unified runner; workflow selection is explicit, and user-supplied parameters are sanitized against workflow input models and protected from any downstream override. Interface locks pin every tool and workflow contract to synchronized documentation and tests; the regression suite comprises 668 passing tests plus 19 end-to-end process-aware tasks.

### Bounded multi-module coordination

For the two coupled workflows, coordination follows a three-phase bounded loop: independent module generation; assembly and evaluation in a normalized objective space (reference-point achievement scalarizing function with Pareto frontier maintenance, front capped at 10 members); and a bounded iteration in which a deterministic coordinator (no LLM) diagnoses gaps against the reference point and broadcasts module-targeted feedback. Bounds include a hard round cap, a convergence threshold (0.05 in normalized ASF space), clamped parameter adjustments (λ ∈ [0, 20]; hotspot window ∈ [3, 10] residues; aptamer length ≤ 200 nt; ≤ 24 new candidates per round), and fixed user reference points across rounds. Type-I (full-length mRNA) coordinates 5′UTR, CDS and 3′UTR at the sequence junctions; Type-II (aptamer) coordinates hotspot selection and ligand generation at the 3D interface. Following the budget-matched self-audit (Results), coordination is retained and documented as an auditable budget-allocation and resampling mechanism, not as a source of claimed coordination gains.

### Calibrated-agency stack

The SelfModel maps each (oracle, task type, target family) tuple to one of four capability grades (screening / ranking / docking / none) with evidence pointers and confidence. The CapabilityProfiler computes grades from anchor-set evaluations against pre-registered thresholds (screening AUROC > 0.8; ranking Spearman ρ ≥ 0.6; docking native top-1 in ≥ 2/3 cases), with a deterministic driver backend. The DecisionGate intercepts planned actions and forbids those whose evidentiary basis is absent (for example, ranking by an oracle with no ranking grade); the ClaimGate intercepts outgoing claims and downgrades them along a scope ladder (absolute → same-target relative → trend-only → suppressed), enforcing "no claim without verification". Both gates are deterministic; every decision is appended to an append-only JSONL audit trail. Anchor sets are assertion-curated: entries carry role labels (binder, negative, unrankable), internal consistency assertions and negative assertions are checked at build time, and coverage (≥ 4 binders and ≥ 3 negatives per target) is verified before use.

### Generation-layer constraint navigation (GenNav)

GenNav comprises four components: (1) a feasibility-first adaptive λ probe over {0, 2, 8, 20, 50} that determines which generator gears are feasible for a target before budget allocation, with an explicit degrade path when no gear is feasible; (2) profile-orthogonal generation axes (CAI-maximizing, fractional, floor) so that design profiles are separable by construction; (3) a diversity expander enforcing ≥ 3% pairwise edit distance within delivered batches; and (4) explicit codon-context resolution, passing species-matched codon tables (for example, *E. coli* for GFP) through to the generators. Ranking is mechanized: the system enforces utility order and the LLM arbitrates ties only. Constraint matrices are pre-registered per campaign (the E5/E6 matrix: GC content windows, homopolymer runs < 14 bp, initiation-region stems ≤ 12 bp, full-length stems ≤ 30 bp, boundary GC, CAI ≥ 0.8).

### Benchmarks, anchors and experimental campaigns

Four benchmarks were used: a 210-task platform suite across the eleven workflows; a 20-target full-length mRNA suite; a 20-target aptamer suite; and a 19-task process-aware multi-turn regression suite (also serving as CI). External anchors include BNT162b2/mRNA-1273 full-length sequences, 20 wild-type CDSs with 200 random synonymous controls, known aptamers (TBA, pegaptanib, SARS-CoV-2 N binders), an IRES positive/negative panel, and small-molecule binder/non-binder pairs. All comparative experiments followed the pilot-first protocol: pre-registration (hypothesis, primary metric, expected effect size, gate, pilot n recorded in the driver), a small-scale pilot with a decisive control arm, a go/no-go gate, and disciplined recording of negative pilots as evidence. The budget-matching rule — equal total generated-and-evaluated candidates across arms for any best-of-pool or hypervolume comparison — was enforced in P0, P3, E4 and E6.

### Statistics

Paired comparisons used two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Cliff's δ as effect size; multiple-testing control used Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. AUROC confidence intervals used 10,000-fold bootstrap. The GenNav primary endpoint used paired McNemar tests on per-profile joint-criteria pass/fail (n = 54 pairs). The memory learning-curve endpoint used a one-sided one-sample t-test on per-seed-group slopes. Thresholds were pre-registered per campaign (for example, δ ≥ 0.3 for pilot GO gates; AUROC > 0.8; ρ ≥ 0.6; FDR q < 0.05 with |δ| ≥ 0.3 for design gains).

### LLM usage and cost reporting

The sole external LLM in all reported results is DeepSeek (deepseek-v4-pro; official endpoint; temperature 0.2 for legacy quality reviews, 0.0 for the in-system LLM under test). Measured usages: platform quality-review suite 93 calls (414,088 prompt + 194,261 completion tokens); gating experiment 80 calls; memory campaign 230 calls; constraint-navigation campaigns 3 (S0), 23 (S1) and 43 (S2) calls. All structure and sequence models run as local microservices with local weights. Experiment orchestration was assisted by the Kimi CLI coding agent, which is not part of the tested system.
